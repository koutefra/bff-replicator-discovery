Replicator Discovery in BFF

What makes execution-driven replicator formation likely?

The bars above say chance alone is a bad way to find a replicator — random sampling gets there eventually, but it takes some 74,000 epochs. A much shorter precursor turns up about ten thousand times more often. Execution never has to find a replicator. It finds the precursor and builds the rest.

BFF programs rewrite one another

BFF is a programming substrate introduced by Agüera y Arcas et al. in Computational Life. It is built on Brainfuck: two 64-byte programs are concatenated and executed as a single 128-cell tape under Brainfuck-style control flow, extended with two movable heads and byte-copy operations. Because code and data share that tape, execution can rewrite the programs themselves. Afterward, the tape is split back into two 64-byte programs.

In the standard BFF soup, two programs are drawn from the population, concatenated, executed, split again, and both resulting programs are returned to the evolving population.

How a program is classified as a replicator

We use the behavioral replication test released with the BFF implementation accompanying Computational Life by Agüera y Arcas et al. A candidate program is paired with 13 independently sampled random partners. Each trial begins with one interaction, then follows the resulting offspring through four additional generations against that trial’s same partner. The stable, unchanging bytes on the parent side and on the offspring side are compared, and the smaller of the two counts becomes the replication score. A candidate that scores at least 60 of 64 is classified as a replicator.

What does execution contribute?

Knierim et al. recently showed that suitably designed stochastic search can match or outperform the standard BFF soup at finding self-replicators. In particular, once the sampling or mutation distribution is chosen to favor useful regions of BFF program space, pairwise BFF interaction is not uniquely efficient as a search strategy.

But this leaves a different question open: why does BFF execution nevertheless accelerate discovery so strongly relative to uniform random sampling, and what does execution actually do when a replicator is created? We address that mechanism directly.

  • Knierim et al.: tuned stochastic search matches the BFF soup
  • So execution isn't uniquely efficient as a search strategy
  • But why is it still far faster than uniform sampling — and what does it do?

Three discovery regimes

The three regimes let us compare two features that are entangled in the standard BFF soup: execution, which can rewrite program code, and population coupling, in which programs interact with other members of the evolving population and both outputs remain in that population. We report time in epochs, using the dynamical regimes as the reference: one epoch is a full round in which every population program participates in exactly one interaction. In U, which has no execution, an epoch denotes the corresponding matched batch of independent samples. R retains the full population-coupled BFF dynamics, while N retains execution but replaces one interaction partner with a fresh uniformly sampled random program.

This lets us ask whether the acceleration in replicator discovery depends on interactions between programs that have jointly evolved in the soup, or whether execution can already exploit structure supplied by random partners.

Watch a replicator form

A similar cross-boundary copying event was illustrated in Computational Life for a single case-study run. We asked how consistently this formation pattern appears across replicator-forming interactions. We reconstructed 100 interactions in which a non-replicating program became a replicator. In 88 of 100, the same formation pathway dominated: concatenating the two programs completed a short copying loop across their boundary, and repeated execution of that loop rewrote 32–64 bytes of the focal program into a replicator in a single interaction. We refer to this cross-boundary copying structure as the pre-replicator loop.

Across all three discovery regimes, every replicator we observed belonged to the same reverse-copy class: it writes a reversed copy of itself onto the other tape, and executing that reversed copy in a later interaction reconstructs the original program. No other replication mechanism was observed.

From loop to replicator

The reconstruction above shows that the dominant formation pathway is not a gradual accumulation of the full nine-instruction core. Instead, a five-instruction pre-replicator fragment on the partner tape (the precursor) combines with one instruction from the target to form the six-instruction pre-replicator loop, and execution then jumps directly from that short structure to the nine-instruction replicator core.

In the representative interaction above, the partner supplies the fragment [ < } , }, the target supplies the closing ], and together they form the pre-replicator loop [ < } , } ]. This is one member of a well-defined class. A loop qualifies when it copies between the two heads, moves both heads by a non-zero net amount per traversal, and contains no arithmetic; the displacements are typically 1 and 2, so repeated traversal walks one head across the target while the other trails behind. Positions and choices vary within those constraints, but not just any five instructions will do. And the fragment cannot loop on its own: it becomes a working copying loop only after concatenation.

This is the important constructive role of BFF execution. On the partner side, the system only needs access to the much shorter pre-replicator fragment; once that fragment meets a suitable target program, execution can perform the larger transformation that creates the replicator.

The fragment is far easier to encounter than a replicator itself. A uniformly sampled program is a replicator with probability 7.1 × 10−11 — 15 detections in 2.1 × 1011 sampled programs. Under a deliberately restrictive estimate, the same program carries a five-instruction pre-replicator fragment with probability 6.6 × 10−7 (the five bytes in this order among 64 positions, everything else neutral), or about once per 1.5 million programs. The fragment is therefore roughly ten thousand times more common than the replicator it helps build. Closing that gap is what execution does.

What limits the pathway?

The mechanism needs two things: a partner carrying a pre-replicator fragment, and a program that partner can activate. Comparing N and R separates them. Both regimes use the same BFF execution rule and create replicators through the same dominant pathway, yet discovery is consistently less frequent in R. N maintains a relatively high discovery-event rate throughout the run, while R reaches its highest rate early, around epochs 2,000–2,500, and remains below N in every time bin.

We measure the two requirements separately.

N R

Are useful partners scarce in R? We hold programs from reconstructed formation events fixed and count candidate partners that can turn at least one of them into a replicator.

The N baseline stays roughly constant. R rises well above it from about epochs 1,500–6,000, reaching its highest availability around epoch 3,500–4,000. After roughly epoch 6,000, availability in R falls below N and remains lower for most of the rest of the run.

R's lower discovery rate therefore occurs despite a substantial period in which it has more qualifying partners than N.

Can the evolving programs use those partners? We reverse the assay: successful partner programs are held fixed, and we measure the fraction of evolving programs that at least one of those partners can activate into a replicator.

Activation is initially similar in the two regimes, with R briefly rising above N. It then drops sharply: around epochs 3,000–5,000, almost none of the tested R programs are activatable. Activation later recovers, but remains below N for the rest of the observed run.

This creates the key contrast: for part of the run, R has more useful partners but fewer activatable programs.

During the interval in which R have more useful partners but still discover fewer replicators, the clearest measured difference is lower activation. Later, both partner availability and activation are lower in R. Supplying the fragment is not sufficient; the program that meets it also has to be activatable.

One possible explanation is disruption during execution. R evolves a much denser population of executable instructions: by epoch 4,000, BFF instructions make up about 20% of bytes in R, compared with less than 8% in N, with strong enrichment of movement (<, >) and copy (,) instructions. These surrounding instructions can move the heads or overwrite bytes before the pre-replicator loop completes its copy trajectory, potentially disrupting an otherwise successful activation.

The constructive role of execution

The self-replicator is not assembled gradually. In a single interaction, a precursor roughly ten thousand times more common than a replicator launches a rewrite that produces one outright. The N regime shows why this matters: its interaction partners are sampled independently, used once, and discarded, yet they repeatedly provide enough structure for execution to finish the job.

But having the precursor is only half of it. It has to land on a program that can be activatable, and that is a property of the population, not of the precursor. R spends part of the run with more qualifying partners than N and still discovers fewer replicators, because what it evolves alongside them is a program distribution that is harder to activate.

So the quantity that governs emergence is not how often useful structure appears. It is how often useful structure appears and meets something it can act on — and a system can push those two in opposite directions.

This works here because the replicators are small: a nine-instruction core reached from a five-instruction precursor. A more complex replicator presumably needs a longer precursor, and longer precursors get rare fast, and a population that supplies them may not stay activatable.

We see this in the emergence phase, before replication is available to propagate anything. So the open question is whether execution alone can keep turning accessible precursors into increasingly complex replicators — building the thing that will eventually take over the work of building.
