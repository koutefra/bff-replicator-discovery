Replicator Discovery in BFF

How does execution turn non-replicating programs into self-replicators?

Both execution regimes discover replicators far sooner than uniform sampling. The faster of the two — where each program executes against a freshly sampled random partner rather than a shared evolving population — reaches first discovery in about half the median time of the standard BFF soup.

BFF programs rewrite one another

BFF is a programming substrate introduced by Agüera y Arcas et al. in Computational Life . It is built on Brainfuck: two 64-byte programs are concatenated and executed as a single 128-cell tape under Brainfuck-style control flow, extended with two movable heads and byte-copy operations. Because code and data share that tape, execution can rewrite the programs themselves. Afterward, the tape is split back into two 64-byte programs.

In the standard BFF soup, two programs are drawn from the population, concatenated, executed, split again, and both resulting programs are returned to the evolving population.

How a program is classified as a replicator

We use the behavioral replication test released with the BFF implementation accompanying Computational Life by Agüera y Arcas et al. A candidate program is paired with 13 independently sampled random partners. Each trial begins with one interaction, then follows the resulting offspring through four additional generations against that trial’s same partner. The stable, unchanging bytes on the parent side and on the offspring side are compared, and the smaller of the two counts becomes the replication score. A candidate that scores at least 60 of 64 is classified as a replicator.

What does execution contribute?

Knierim et al. recently showed that suitably designed stochastic search can match or outperform the standard BFF soup at finding self-replicators. In particular, once the sampling or mutation distribution is chosen to favor useful regions of BFF program space, pairwise BFF interaction is not uniquely efficient as a search strategy.

But this leaves a different question open: why does BFF execution nevertheless accelerate discovery so strongly relative to uniform random sampling, and what does execution actually do when a replicator is created? We address that mechanism directly.

Three discovery regimes

The three regimes separate two effects that are entangled in the standard BFF soup: execution, which can rewrite program code, and population coupling, which feeds the products of those interactions back into the evolving population. We measure time in epochs: one epoch is a complete population update in which every program participates in exactly one interaction. U removes execution entirely, R retains the full population-coupled BFF dynamics, and N retains execution while replacing one interaction partner with a fresh uniformly sampled random program.

This lets us ask whether the acceleration in replicator discovery depends on interactions between programs that have jointly evolved in the soup, or whether execution can already exploit structure supplied by random partners.

Watch a replicator form

A similar cross-boundary copying event was illustrated in Computational Life for a single case-study run. We asked how consistently this formation pattern appears across replicator-forming interactions. We reconstructed 100 interactions in which a non-replicating program became a replicator. In 88 of 100, the same general formation pathway dominated: concatenating the two programs completed a short copying loop across their boundary, and repeated execution of that loop rewrote 32–64 bytes of the focal program into a replicator in a single interaction. We refer to this cross-boundary copying structure as the pre-replicator loop.

Across all three discovery regimes, every replicator we observed belonged to the same reverse-copy class: it writes a reversed copy of itself onto the other tape, and executing that reversed copy in a later interaction reconstructs the original program. No other replication mechanism was observed.

From loop to replicator

The reconstruction above shows that the dominant formation pathway is not a gradual accumulation of the full nine-instruction core. Instead, a five-instruction partner fragment combines with one instruction from the target to form a six-instruction copying loop, and execution then jumps directly from that short structure to the nine-instruction replicator core.

This is the important constructive role of BFF execution. On the partner side, the system only needs access to the much shorter five-instruction fragment; once that fragment meets a suitable target program, execution can perform the larger transformation that creates the replicator.

That short fragment is also relatively easy to encounter. Even under a deliberately restrictive estimate, it occurs about once per 1.5 million uniformly sampled programs — roughly an 8% chance of encountering at least one in a soup-sized batch of 217 programs. In N, fresh random partners can therefore supply this structure repeatedly over the course of a run, without the partner population having to evolve toward a complete replicator. By N's median first-discovery time, about 53 occurrences of this one canonical fragment are expected in the relevant interaction orientation.

Why is R slower than N?

N and R use the same BFF execution rule and usually create replicators through the same dominant pathway, yet discovery is consistently less frequent in R. N maintains a relatively high discovery-event rate throughout the run, while R reaches its highest rate early, around epochs 2,000–2,500, and remains below N in every time bin.

The dominant pathway requires two things: a useful partner and a program that can be converted by it. We measure these two requirements separately.

N R

Are useful partners scarce in R? We hold programs from reconstructed formation events fixed and count candidate partners that can turn at least one of them into a replicator.

The N baseline stays roughly constant. R rises well above it from about epochs 1,500–6,000, reaching its highest availability around epoch 3,500–4,000. After roughly epoch 6,000, availability in R falls below N and remains lower for most of the rest of the run.

R's lower discovery rate therefore occurs despite a substantial period in which it has more qualifying partners than N.

Can the evolving programs use those partners? We reverse the assay: successful partner programs are held fixed, and we measure the fraction of evolving programs that at least one of those partners can convert into a replicator.

Activation is initially similar in the two regimes, with R briefly rising above N. It then drops sharply: around epochs 3,000–5,000, almost none of the tested R programs are activatable. Activation later recovers, but remains below N for the rest of the observed run.

This creates the key contrast: for part of the run, R has more useful partners but less activatable programs.

R can have more useful partners and still discover fewer replicators. During this interval, the clearest measured difference is lower activation. Later, both partner availability and activation are lower in R.

One possible explanation is disruption during execution. R evolves a much denser population of executable instructions: by epoch 4,000, BFF instructions make up about 20% of bytes in R, compared with less than 8% in N, with strong enrichment of movement (<, >) and copy (,) instructions. These surrounding instructions can move the heads or overwrite bytes before the pre-replicator loop completes its copy trajectory, potentially disrupting an otherwise successful conversion. This provides a plausible mechanism, but the causal link has not been isolated directly.

The constructive role of execution

Across 88 of 100 reconstructed replicator-forming interactions, a short copying loop completed across the interaction boundary launches a copying trajectory that rewrites 32–64 bytes and produces a functional reverse-copy replicator in a single execution.

The N regime shows why this matters for the discovery of self-replicators. Its interaction partners are sampled independently, used once, and discarded, yet they repeatedly provide enough structure for execution to complete the transformation. The complete replicator structure therefore does not need to be present before execution: a much shorter precursor can launch the transformation that constructs it.

How far this mechanism scales remains open. The reverse-copy replicators studied here have a very short functional core, and the precursor structures required for more complex replicators may become much harder to encounter. Importantly, we observe this mechanism in the emergence phase, before self-replication has become an established population-level process. This raises a broader question: can execution dynamics continue to turn relatively accessible precursor structures into increasingly complex self-replicators before replication itself is available to drive their propagation and refinement?
